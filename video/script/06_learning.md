# 06 — Energy-based training: why it works, and why it first did not
voice: kokoro
length-target: ~12 min

## p01 | viz: loop diagram, Learning box lit. The arrow in: r from the body. The arrow out: Δθ to the brain. On screen: "119 knobs in this brain, one number per tick". A strip of 60 real r values from day 0 of seed 203: mostly small red, one green bite.
Fifth box: learning. The need: turn a hundred and nineteen knobs, in this brain after it grew, so that the body's number drops more often. The brain never sees food. It sees this: a stream of lessons, most of them slightly negative, a few of them positive, and it must find which knobs to turn. Two rules were tried. The first is the contrastive policy-gradient rule, the energy-based classic, and it failed. The second is the value rule, and it worked. You need both to understand the result.

## p02 | viz: RULE ONE, the shape. Δθ = lr · R · (slope of something about the move taken). The three price tags; the taken one marked.
Rule one. Its shape is: the change in a knob is the learning rate, times the lesson, times the slope of something about the move that was taken. If the lesson was good, push the move taken toward more likely. If bad, push it away. The whole question is: the slope of what?

## p03 | viz: WHY THE LOG. Two curves against energy of the taken move: the chance π itself, and log π. For a rare move (π = 0.02) the slope of π is nearly flat; the slope of log π stays usable. The equation: slope of log π = slope of π, divided by π.
Why the slope of the log of the chance, and not the slope of the chance itself? Watch the alternative fail. A move the brain rarely takes has a tiny chance, and the chance's slope is tiny too, so the rule could never learn about rare moves, however good their lessons. The log divides that slope by the chance, and undoes exactly that. Every move, common or rare, gets a usable push.

## p04 | viz: THE TWO PHASES, moving. log π(a|x) = Q(x,a)/T − log Σ exp(Q(x,a′)/T), its slope splitting into two bracketed terms. Beside it, the three energy bars. As "positive phase" is spoken the taken bar sinks by lr·R; as "negative phase" is spoken all three bars rise, each by its own chance times the same amount, the most likely one rising most. The net movement shown as the difference.
Expand the log of the softmax, and its slope splits in two. The first part: lower the energy of the move that was taken, in proportion to the lesson. That is the positive phase. Watch the taken bar sink. The second part: raise the energy of every move, each in proportion to the chance the brain itself gave it, the average from chapter two. That is the negative phase. Watch all three rise, the favourite most. The brain's own habit is pushed up, what the body liked is pushed down, and the difference is what moves.

## p05 | viz: TOY, computed live and labelled "toy, not lab data". Three moves, every lesson = +1 whatever the move. Left panel: positive phase only: the chosen bar sinks, the most-chosen sinks most, the policy collapses onto a random favourite. Right panel: both phases: single steps jitter, the running average stays flat.
Why is the negative phase there? Watch it missing. Three moves, and a body that says plus one no matter what. With the positive phase alone, every move taken gets its energy lowered, so the move taken most often sinks most, gets taken even more, and the brain collapses onto a favourite that was never better than the others. Praise without comparison. Add the negative phase, and when the lesson does not depend on the move, the two phases cancel on average and the policy stays where it is. The rule only learns differences between moves.

## p06 | viz: THE CREDITED LESSON. The strip of 60 real r from day 0. R_t = Σ λ^k r_{t+k} with λ = 0.95: the bite's credit smears backward over ~20 ticks. Then the mean is subtracted; then the day is divided by its spread, and the axis relabels to "about one".
The lesson that enters rule one is not the raw r. Three things are done to it. First, every tick is credited with the lessons that followed it, each shrunk by a factor called lambda, zero point nine five per tick, so the turn that led to a bite twenty ticks later gets a share of the bite. Lambda is a cousin of the gamma you will meet in rule two: same job, counting far lessons less, in a rule that has no promise to carry them. Second, the day's average is subtracted, because metabolism makes every tick slightly negative and a constant teaches nothing. Third, the day is divided by its own spread, so the lessons become numbers of size about one. Why about one? Because at temperature zero point three the softmax only cares about gaps of that size, chapter two. And that size-one lesson, multiplied by the learning rate and divided by the temperature, is where the trouble started.

## p07 | viz: STEP VERSUS BRAIN, nothing else on screen. The knobs at birth as a short bar of length 0.09 (the length card from chapter two). The night-1 step as a bar 0.68 to 1.39 long.
Here is what happened in your first experiment, measured. The knobs at birth have a length of about zero point zero nine. The first night's step had a length between zero point six eight and one point three nine. Eight to sixteen times the whole brain, in one night.

## p08 | viz: THE LURCH. From m3_bias.json: seed 313's resting turn bias night by night, +0.99 then −1.00. Then the other proof seeds' lines, flipping.
After that night the policy was almost deterministic: on the four proof seeds it gave one move more than ninety-six percent of the time, and on two of them more than ninety-nine. And it did not settle. Seed three one three went from turning left with ninety-nine percent confidence to turning right with a hundred percent, overnight. Not a stall, a lurch.

## p09 | viz: ONE KNOB. The policy-gradient step written with its two 1/T factors: one from the slope of log π, one from how sharply the chances react to an energy change; they combine into lr/T². A dial: halve T, the step quadruples.
Your first diagnosis said the brain learned too weakly to act. The referees measured it and the record now says the opposite, with the wrong sentence kept in place and corrected underneath. One more thing they found: the step size and the temperature are one knob. One over T comes from the slope of the log chance. A second one over T comes from how sharply the chances react to a change in energy. Together the policy moves as the learning rate over T squared, so halving T is the same as quadrupling the step. Lowering one or raising the other both kept the policy sane.

## p10 | viz: the hill from chapter one: the dot takes a step 10× the valley width, lands on the far slope, steps back, lurches; beside it a small step walks down. A label: "the daily gradient pointed the right way on 96% of creature-days".
The picture is the one from chapter one. Take a step ten times wider than the valley, and you land on the opposite slope, every time, in a new place. The signal was fine: the daily gradient on the key knob pointed the right way on ninety-six percent of creature-days, about twice as much signal as noise per day. The step wasted it.

## p11 | viz: RULE TWO. Q(x,a) on the left; on the right, "r now + γ · best Q next". The promise written as an equation with ≈. A path of ticks: a bite at the end; γ^k shown shrinking: 0.9^20 = 0.12, 0.9^50 = 0.005.
Rule two, the one that worked. Q makes a promise: the value of this move here should equal the lesson I get now, plus gamma times the best value I can expect from where I land. Why the lesson now? That is the part that already happened. Why the best next value, the max? Because the promise assumes you will keep choosing well from there. Why gamma, zero point nine? Because a bite twenty ticks away is less certain than one now; at zero point nine, twenty ticks away counts twelve percent, fifty ticks away half a percent. Planning a day out, you care about the next hour more than the evening, but you do not ignore the evening.

## p12 | viz: PAUSE AND PREDICT: "γ → 0.99. How far does the promise reach?" A chain of 60 spots with a bite at the end: at γ 0.9 the value fades out after ~20 spots; at 0.99 it reaches the whole chain (0.99^20 = 0.82); at 0.5 it dies after 3 spots (0.5^3 = 0.125).
Pause and predict. Raise gamma to zero point nine nine. How far does the promise reach? Twenty ticks away now counts eighty-two percent, and the smell of food fifty ticks out is still worth something. Lower it to zero point five and the creature only cares about the next three ticks: a bite it cannot reach in three moves is worth almost nothing to it. Gamma is how far ahead the creature lives.

## p13 | viz: THE UPDATE. y = r + γ·max Q(x′); miss = y − Q(x,a); knob += learning rate · miss · reading. The real tick 3693 at full precision: Q(x, LEFT) = +0.001089, r = −0.00026, y = +0.000892, miss = y − Q = −0.000197. Then Δw[LEFT] = 0.3 × miss × x: −0.000031 on contrast A, −0.000054 on energy, −0.000059 on the constant. Only the LEFT row of w and b lights, plus the committee's shared c and u.
The update is the slope rule from chapter two. Compute the promise's right-hand side, call it y. The miss is y minus Q. Turn each knob by the miss, times the knob's reading, times the learning rate, zero point three. The code calls that rate alpha q, a different alpha from the smell's alpha in chapter three; they share a letter and nothing else. Only the taken move's own knobs move, plus the committee's shared input knobs; the rows that belong to the other two moves stay put. Real numbers, tick three six nine three. Q for left was plus zero point zero zero one one. The lesson was minus twenty-six hundred-thousandths. The best next Q was about plus zero point zero zero one three, so y is plus zero point zero zero zero nine, and the miss is minus two ten-thousandths. Small. Each left knob moves by the miss times its reading: a few hundred-thousandths. The promise was nearly right already.

## p15 | viz: WHY CENTRING MATTERED (A16). Uncentred: all three Q at 2.8; the target y = r + 0.9 × 2.8 ≈ 2.5 drawn a quarter below; each step pulls only the taken bar down; the greedy move loses its top spot after its own update 64–69% of the time; the creature rotates through moves. Centred: all three near zero, the target near zero, the steps tiny.
Now the centring from chapter five. Without it, every Q started at about two point eight. The target is the lesson plus nine tenths of the next Q, so with every Q sitting at two point eight the target is always about a quarter below it, and every step pulled only the taken move down. The move on top lost its top spot right after its own update two times in three. The creature rotated through moves instead of choosing. A referee caught it before the main run; on the pilot seeds the fix took one arm from six hundred ticks to thirty-seven hundred.

## p16 | viz: CONSISTENCY. A chain of five spots, food at the end. All Q start at 0. Pass 1: only the last spot learns the bite. Pass 2: the spot before it learns 0.9 × that. Passes 3, 4, 5: the value walks backward down the chain. The bite is the only anchor.
Notice that the target y contains Q itself. The brain is chasing a target that moves as it learns. Why does that settle instead of running in circles? Because of the anchor. The only place the promise is pinned to something real is where the body speaks: the bite. Watch a chain of five spots with food at the end. First pass, only the last spot learns the bite. Second pass, the spot before it learns nine tenths of that. Each pass, the truth walks one step back along the chain. This is called bootstrapping. Like news of a prize at the end of a corridor: the person next to it knows first, then tells the next, one handover per visit. That is the whole reason value learning works, and why it needs many visits.

## p17 | viz: epsilon-greedy and the sign. The three Q bars with gaps of a few thousandths; the highest is picked; the dice fires on 1 in 10 ticks. A small label: "the target uses max, so it learns about the best move even while doing a random one".
Why does this work with knob differences of a few thousandths? Because the promise is compared, not scaled. Taking the highest Q reads the sign of a difference, and three thousandths has a sign. The dice, one time in ten, keeps the other two moves visited so their promises get checked too. And because the target uses the best next move, not the move actually taken, the brain learns about choosing well even on the ticks it chose randomly. The random moves need no special treatment. That is what off-policy means, and it is one less thing to get wrong.

## p18 | viz: ONLINE + NIGHT. Day: one update per tick on the live transition. Night: the last 3 days' transitions (~900) shuffled, cut into batches of 64, three passes. A bite's transition is highlighted and reused 9 times.
When does it learn? Every waking tick, one step on the transition that just happened. And every night, replay: the last three days of transitions, about nine hundred, shuffled together, cut into batches of sixty-four, and walked three times. Why replay? A bite is a handful of ticks in a day of three hundred; replay lets each one teach nine times instead of once. Why shuffle? Consecutive ticks are near copies of each other, and a batch of near copies is one lesson pretending to be sixty-four. Shuffled, each batch is a sample of the whole day. Why batches at all? The average of sixty-four slopes is a steadier arrow than one.

## p19 | viz: DOWNSCALE and GROWTH. w_norm per night from run.json: 0.096 after night 0 → 0.078 after night 14, a gentle slide. The grow event on night 1: a fifth unit appears with v = 0, its three output bars at zero.
Two more things happen at night. All connection knobs shrink by one percent, so nothing can grow without bound and old lessons fade a little. You can see it: the brain's knob length slides from zero point zero nine six after the first night to zero point zero seven eight after the last. And if the day was eventful, if the spread of the day's lessons was above a threshold, the brain grows one unit. The new unit is born with its three output knobs at zero, so on the night it is born it changes no choice. Seed two hundred and three grew exactly once, on its second night, and never had another day eventful enough.

## p20 | viz: THE TWO RULES SIDE BY SIDE. Rule one: lesson standardised to size 1, times lr 1, divided by T 0.3 (a factor of 3.3), ~300 samples on night 1 in ~15 batch steps of 0.08–0.19 each, on knobs of total length 0.09. Rule two: lesson in raw hundredths × 0.3 → steps of hundred-thousandths on knob differences of thousandths.
Why did rule two not lurch where rule one did? Same brain, same world, same body. Rule one scaled every lesson up to size one and then divided by the temperature, zero point three, a factor of three point three, so each batch moved the knobs by about a tenth, on a brain whose knobs were a tenth in total. Rule two regresses the raw lesson, hundredths, with a step of zero point three, so each step moves the knobs by hundred-thousandths on a quiet tick, and by a few hundredths at most on a bite tick, on knob differences of thousandths. One rule's step was small enough for its targets, just, and one was not. The referees added a caution: the Q step is itself at the edge of stability, and the arm with fifteen readings instead of twelve blew up on night zero because of it. That is why your findings say rule two worked, and not that it is safe.

## p21 | viz: the loop closes: Learning → Brain arrow labelled "learning rate · miss · reading". Then the one-line collapse on screen.
Connections. The lesson comes from the body. The slope comes from the brain's formula, and for a weighted sum it is the reading. The promise comes from the brain's own next Q. Stripped down: Q is a promise, and learning is keeping every promise consistent with what the body said and with the next promise. The bite is the only anchor, and the chain walks back from it, one step per visit.
