## p01 | viz: title "Maths from the beginning". Seven small cards: weighted sum, exponent, softmax, log, slope, average, length. A one-line caption at the bottom, on screen only, not spoken: "voice from here: Kokoro (local); the paid minutes went to chapters 0 and 1".
Seven operations. Everything in the brain is built from them. For each one: the need it answers, why this operation and not another, one example from life, and then the same operation on the creature's real numbers.

## p02 | viz: WEIGHTED SUM. The twelve readings of tick 3693 in a column, the twelve LEFT knobs the brain used at that tick beside them (theta_at), products in a third column, the total 0.014248 at the bottom. The two biggest products (body_E 0.00864, the constant 0.00288) glow.
Weighted sum. The need: twelve readings, one evidence for the move "left". Why multiply first? Because each reading matters a different amount, and in a different direction. Why add? Because we want the total vote. The grocery bill from chapter one: quantity times price, added up. Here it is on the real tick, three six nine three, day ten. Twelve products, one total: about fourteen thousandths. Most of it comes from the energy reading and the constant one. The committee and the resting preference, coming in chapter five, then bring the final Q for left down to about a thousandth. Keep that in mind: the sign of a difference will matter more than the size.

## p03 | viz: the alternatives failing. Left: plain sum of readings (no knobs): the total cannot tell A from C. Right: max of readings: eleven readings are ignored.
Why not something simpler? If you just add the readings with no knobs, every smell counts the same, and the brain can never prefer A over C. If you take only the biggest reading, you throw away eleven of twelve. The weighted sum is the smallest thing that can weigh every reading differently and still give one number.

## p04 | viz: EXPONENT, the need. The three Q of tick 3693. Try "odds = Q": one bar is negative, a crossed-out ticket. Try "odds = Q squared": then add the same 0.01 to all three Q and the shares change, although nothing between the moves changed.
Exponent. The need: turn evidences into odds, tickets in a raffle. Watch two obvious choices fail on the real tick. Odds equal to Q: two of the three Q are negative, and you cannot hold minus two tickets. Odds equal to Q squared: always positive, but now add the same small amount to all three Q, and the shares change, although nothing between the moves changed. The brain's evidences only mean something as differences, so a rule that reacts to a shared shift is wrong.

## p05 | viz: the exponent passes both tests: a number line of Q; exp maps it to a positive axis; two Q that differ by 1 map to odds that differ by a factor of e; slide the pair along the line, the factor stays.
The exponent passes both. It is always positive. And e to the a plus b equals e to the a times e to the b, so a difference in evidence becomes a ratio in odds, and a shared shift cancels in the ratio. Compound interest does this: each year multiplies your money by the same factor, so a fixed number of years is a fixed ratio, whichever year you start.

## p06 | viz: WHY e. The curve e^x with its tangent; the slope at every point equals the height. A small label "slope = value".
Why the number e and not two or ten? Any base would give ratios. e is the base whose curve has a slope equal to its own height. That makes every slope we take later come out clean, with no extra constant hanging off it. With e, lowering an energy by one unit at temperature one multiplies that move's odds by two point seven one eight. Not a magic number, a convenient one.

## p07 | viz: SOFTMAX, built in four steps on the real Q of tick 3693: (1) the three Q bars, (2) divide by T, (3) exp of each, (4) divide each by the sum. The final three bars are the chances.
Softmax. The need: three evidences, and we want three chances that add up to one. Four steps. Take the Q. Divide them by the temperature. Take e to the power of each. Then divide each by the total of all three. That last step is the raffle: your chance is your tickets divided by everyone's tickets. Here it is on the real tick. The three Q are plus zero point zero zero one one, minus zero point zero zero two three, minus zero point zero zero one six.

## p08 | viz: PAUSE AND PREDICT card: "T = 0.3. What are the three chances?" Hold. Then reveal: 0.336, 0.332, 0.333. Then slide T down to 0.05: 0.347, 0.324, 0.329. Then to 0.001: LEFT rises to 0.907.
Pause and predict. The temperature in your lab is zero point three. What does softmax give for these three Q? Have a guess. Here is the answer: thirty-three point six, thirty-three point two, thirty-three point three percent. Almost equal. The gaps between the Q are a few thousandths, and dividing by zero point three does not stretch them enough to matter. Even at T equals zero point zero five it barely moves. To make softmax pick left ninety percent of the time, T would need to be about one thousandth.

## p09 | viz: T as a dial. The three bars as T sweeps from 0.001 to 1: from one tall bar to three equal bars, smoothly. Beside it, the epsilon dice: one in ten, whatever the bars.
So what does T do, in one sentence: dividing by T stretches or squashes the gaps before the exponent sees them. Small T, gaps look huge, one move wins. Large T, gaps look tiny, every move is equally likely. The marble and the shaking table, now as arithmetic. You could run the second experiment at T equals a thousandth. But then how often the creature explores would depend on the gaps, which change all life. The dice, epsilon, fixes exploring at one in ten whatever the gaps. One less thing to tune, and that is what the winning brain uses.

## p10 | viz: LOG. A smell axis from 0 to 5, and the reading log1p(c/0.05) above it. Points at c = 0.05, 0.5, 5 with readings 0.693, 2.398, 4.615. Beside it, the raw-concentration alternative: near zero far away, a spike near the source. Then a square-root curve: doubling steps shrink; on the log curve every doubling step is the same 0.693.
Log. The need: the nose must hear a whisper and a shout. Two cells from a food source the smell is already five times weaker, four cells away twenty times weaker, and across the map it spans about a thousandfold. If the brain read the raw smell, everything far away would round to zero and everything near would blow out. The log fixes it: every doubling of the smell adds the same amount to the reading, zero point six nine. That is how decibels work for your ears. Why not a square root, which also squashes? Because under a square root, doubling adds a different amount at every level. A smell of zero point zero five reads zero point six nine. Ten times stronger reads two point four. A hundred times stronger reads four point six: a hundred times louder is under seven times bigger on the reading.

## p11 | viz: log1p detail: log(c) dives to minus infinity at zero; log(1 + c/c0) passes gently through zero. The constant c0 = 0.05 slides and the curve's knee moves.
One detail. The plain log of zero is minus infinity, and a creature in clean air would feel infinite nothing. So the code uses log of one plus the smell divided by a constant. No smell gives exactly zero. The constant, zero point zero five, is where the curve bends from roughly linear to roughly log. That is the only knob in the log.

## p12 | viz: SLOPE, one knob. A curve of mistake versus one knob value. A dot on it; the slope as a small triangle, rise over run. The dot steps against the slope and settles at the bottom.
Slope. The need: the brain made a mistake; which way should it turn each knob? One knob first. Draw the mistake against the knob's value: a curve with a bottom. The slope at your point is rise over run: how much the mistake changes per unit of knob. If the slope is positive, turning the knob up makes things worse, so turn it down. Move against the slope, a little, and check again. That is a shower tap: too hot, turn toward cold, a bit, feel again. The mistake, from chapter six on, we will call the miss, and the code calls it td.

## p13 | viz: GRADIENT, many knobs. "evidence = w·x, the simplest brain" written out; the slope of the evidence with respect to knob k is x_k. Each knob's arrow is drawn with length equal to its reading. Then the nudge on the real tick: +0.1 on the LEFT knob for contrast A moves Q by +0.0521; +0.1 on the knob for body X moves it by +0.00014.
Many knobs is the same thing, one slope per knob, and the list of slopes is the gradient. Here is the one fact you need about our brain's gradient, ignoring the committee for now. The evidence is a weighted sum, knob times reading, added up. So the slope of the evidence with respect to one knob is just that knob's reading. Watch it on the real tick. Nudge the left knob on the A contrast by a tenth: Q for left moves by a tenth times zero point five two, five hundredths. Nudge the knob on the toxin reading by the same tenth: Q moves by a tenth times zero point zero zero one, nothing. A knob can only matter through the reading it multiplies. That is why the brain only learns about smells it has actually smelled.

## p14 | viz: EXPECTATION. Three outcomes with chances 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and values 10, −5, 0. Each value is scaled by its chance and the pieces are stacked into one bar: 5 − 1.5 + 0 = 3.5.
Average over chances. The need, in two places: the brain's guess of what it would do anyway, and the value of a spot, which is an average over futures. The operation: each outcome times its chance, added up. A lottery ticket's worth is the prize times the chance of winning, summed over prizes. Ten with chance one half, minus five with chance three tenths, zero with chance one fifth: five, minus one and a half, plus nothing, three and a half. When you see a sum with a probability inside it later, it is this.

## p15 | viz: LENGTH. Twelve knobs as arrows of different lengths, some negative. Each is squared into an area; the areas add into one area; the square root turns it back into one arrow of that side length. Slide one knob and the arrow grows. Then the real pair: a step of 0.68 beside a brain of 0.09.
Length. The need: one number for how big a whole list of knobs is, so we can say a step was eight times the brain. Why square each knob? So the sign does not matter and big knobs count more. Why add? Total size. Why the square root at the end? To undo the squaring, so the answer is back in knob units. This is the same recipe as the distance across a room from its two sides. A step of zero point six eight on a brain of zero point zero nine is a step eight times the brain. That is the number from chapter one, and you will meet it again in chapter six.

## p16 | viz: the seven cards collapse into one line each; then the loop diagram with Brain and Learning lit: "weighted sum, exponent, softmax, log live in Brain and Senses; slope, average, length live in Learning".
Strip it back down. A weighted sum is a total vote. The exponent turns gaps into ratios. Softmax is my tickets divided by all the tickets. A log makes every doubling add the same amount. The slope of a weighted sum is the knob's reading. An average is value times chance, summed. Length is square, add, root. None of these is a box on the loop; the first four live inside the brain and the senses, the last three inside learning.
